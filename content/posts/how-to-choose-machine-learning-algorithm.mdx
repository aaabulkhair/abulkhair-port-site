Every ML engagement starts with some version of the same question: *"which algorithm should we use?"* After years of shipping models, my answer has become boringly consistent: **you don't choose the algorithm — your data's profile narrows it to a family, and your constraints pick the winner.** Here is how to choose a machine learning algorithm the way practitioners actually do it: read the data first, shortlist a model family, then let latency, storage, and explainability requirements make the final call.

## The only question that matters: splits or surfaces?

Every supervised algorithm does the same job — it draws a boundary between classes (or a surface through your target). What separates algorithms is the *shape* of boundary they can draw. Look at what seven popular algorithms do to the same three toy datasets:

![Decision boundaries of seven machine learning algorithms on three datasets, showing how to choose a machine learning algorithm by boundary shape](/images/posts/how-to-choose-machine-learning-algorithm/decision-boundaries.png "Same data, seven algorithms — logistic regression and linear SVM draw straight lines, trees draw boxes, KNN follows neighborhoods, RBF SVM and neural networks draw curves.")

Read the grid row by row and the whole selection problem comes into focus:

- **Logistic regression and linear SVM** draw straight lines, always. On the moons and circles datasets they fail no matter how you tune them — the boundary they need doesn't exist in their vocabulary.
- **Decision trees and random forests** draw axis-aligned boxes. They approximate the circles dataset with a staircase of splits — crude for one tree, smoother for a forest.
- **KNN** traces the local neighborhood structure — flexible, but jagged and easily distracted by noise and irrelevant features.
- **RBF SVM and neural networks** draw smooth curves and handle every dataset well.

So the first question to ask of your data is: *can my classes be separated by a smooth linear trend, or do I need splits and curves?* That answer eliminates half the algorithms before you've trained anything.

One precision worth stating, because it's widely muddled: **"linear model" means linear in *your* input features** — a weighted sum of the columns you feed in, full stop. A neural network is not a linear model wearing a trench coat; it's a *composition* of linear maps and non-linear activations, which is exactly why it can draw the curves above. Likewise, an RBF SVM is linear only in an implicit, infinite-dimensional feature space it never shows you — in the space where your features actually live, its boundary is curved. If "linear in *some* transformed space" counted, every model would be linear and the word would mean nothing.

## The four families (and where Naive Bayes actually lives)

Group algorithms by how they draw boundaries and the zoo becomes manageable:

![The four families of machine learning algorithms: linear in the input features, tree-based, distance-based, and learned feature space](/images/posts/how-to-choose-machine-learning-algorithm/model-family-map.png "Four families, four ways to draw a boundary — with probabilistic models like Naive Bayes sitting outside the map.")

- **Linear in the input features** — linear regression, logistic regression, linear SVM. Fast to train, fast to serve, fully interpretable, and hard to beat when the signal is genuinely additive. They're my default baseline in every project.
- **Tree-based** — decision trees, random forests, and the gradient boosting family (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost). Splits don't care about feature scale, outliers, or non-normal distributions, and boosting is still the benchmark to beat on tabular data.
- **Distance-based** — KNN and friends. Predictions follow the closest training points, so the distance metric *is* the model. Powerful in low dimensions with meaningful geometry; falls apart as dimensions grow.
- **Learned feature space** — neural networks and kernel SVMs. They manufacture their own internal features and are linear *there*. That buys expressive power at the cost of data hunger, tuning, and opacity.

Naive Bayes sits outside this map: it's **probabilistic**, scoring each class with Bayes' theorem under a conditional-independence assumption. (A nice detail for interviews: with Gaussian, equal-variance features, Naive Bayes is linear in the log-odds — closer to logistic regression than to KNN. The common habit of filing it under "distance-based" is just wrong.)

## Let EDA pick your shortlist

Exploratory data analysis is not a ritual you perform before the real work — it *is* the model selection step. Three findings do most of the deciding:

**Outliers.** A linear model chases outliers — squared loss literally squares their influence. If your box plots show heavy tails, either clean them deliberately or lean tree-based; splits isolate outliers into their own leaves and move on. Clipping helps linear models but destroys information; log or square-root transforms dampen outliers *without* losing it, which is why I reach for transforms first.

**Distribution and correlation.** One correction to a claim you'll see everywhere (including, once, from me): linear regression does **not** assume your *data* is normally distributed — it assumes the *errors* are, and only for valid confidence intervals at that. What actually matters for model choice is the shape of the feature–target relationship. Strong, monotone correlations between features and target favor linear models — they'll capture the signal with two coefficients where a tree needs fifty splits. Weak, interaction-heavy, or non-monotone relationships favor trees.

**Missing values.** Ask *why* they're missing before deciding how to fill them. Missingness tied to a real-world event ("income missing = declined to disclose") is signal — encode it as its own category and let a tree split on it. Random missingness favors imputation: mean/median for linear models, where preserving the marginal distribution matters. Some models handle NaNs natively — XGBoost routes them down learned default branches, and Naive Bayes simply skips the missing feature's likelihood (though sklearn's implementation won't do this for you; it rejects NaNs outright).

## Feature engineering follows the family

The preprocessing that helps one family is wasted on another. The rules I actually follow:

- **Scaling:** tree-based models are indifferent — splits compare values within a single feature, so scale is irrelevant. Distance-based models *require* it, or your largest-magnitude feature silently dominates every distance. Linear models sit in between: scaling doesn't change what an unregularized OLS fit can express, but it dramatically speeds up gradient-based solvers and is non-negotiable once regularization enters, because the penalty treats large-scale coefficients as more important.
- **Encoding categoricals:** one-hot encoding suits linear models — each category gets its own weight, no fake ordering imposed. Frequency encoding compresses high-cardinality features into one informative column. Tree-based models happily consume label-encoded or natively categorical features (CatBoost, LightGBM), and target encoding captures category–target relationships directly — just fit it on out-of-fold data or you'll leak.
- **Imputation:** an explicit "Unknown" category for categoricals favors trees; mean/median favors linear models. Match the strategy to the family, not to a blog-post default.

Get the family right and much of "feature engineering" becomes obvious. Get it wrong and you'll spend a week tuning around a mismatch that a different algorithm would have made disappear.

## The constraints that overrule the data

Sometimes the data points one way and reality points the other. Reality wins.

- **Latency:** scoring a logistic regression is a dot product — microseconds. A deep ensemble or a large boosted model costs orders of magnitude more. In real-time systems (fraud scoring on the swipe, ad bidding, driver assistance), the "worse" model that answers in time beats the better one that doesn't.
- **Storage and memory:** a random forest with 500 deep trees or a KNN that must carry the entire training set into production can be a genuine deployment problem on edge devices or serverless functions.
- **Interpretability:** regulated industries and planner-facing tools often *require* an explanation a human can audit. That single requirement can take neural networks off the table before accuracy enters the discussion.

Which leads to the trade-off no dataset can settle for you:

![Interpretability versus predictive power trade-off across machine learning algorithms from linear regression to neural networks](/images/posts/how-to-choose-machine-learning-algorithm/interpretability-tradeoff.png "White-box models explain every prediction; black-box models buy accuracy with opacity. Gradient boosting is the usual sweet spot on structured data.")

Notice where gradient boosting sits: near the top of predictive power on structured data, with SHAP values giving you a decent (if post-hoc) explanation. That's why it's the workhorse of applied ML — and why "just use a neural network" is usually the wrong answer on tabular data.

## My default playbook

1. **Profile the data first.** Distributions, outliers, missingness, feature–target correlations. Thirty minutes here saves a week later.
2. **Fit a linear baseline.** Logistic or linear regression with sensible preprocessing. It's the number everyone has to beat, and often nobody does.
3. **Fit a gradient boosting contender.** LightGBM or XGBoost with light tuning. If it doesn't clearly beat the baseline on a proper validation split, ship the baseline.
4. **Reach for a neural network only when it's earned** — unstructured data (text, images, audio), or a tabular problem where boosting has demonstrably plateaued and you have data volume to spare.
5. **Let constraints break ties.** Latency budget, model size, explainability requirements, retraining cadence. The best model is the best model you can actually run.
6. **Evaluate like a skeptic.** Time-aware or stratified splits, a metric tied to the business cost of being wrong, and a calibration check if anyone will act on the probabilities.

The algorithm is the visible 10% of the decision. The other 90% — reading the data honestly, preprocessing for the family you chose, and respecting deployment reality — is what separates models that ship from models that demo.
